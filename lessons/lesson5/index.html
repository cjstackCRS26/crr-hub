Understand the Power of Academics: The Part You Control

By Coach Stack · College Recruiting Standard

The part of recruiting you control most

You can't control how tall you grow, which coach shows up to your game, or how many players a college needs at your position. You can control your grades. That's why Step 5 opens Level III, Pathways: academics are the engine behind every pathway, in sport and beyond it.

There's a reason it's called student-athlete, with “student” first. Every college coach has to ask three questions about you, and two of them are about the classroom.

Three doors your grades open

DoorThe questionWhat decides it
EligibilityCan you compete?NCAA Division I and II, and the NAIA, set academic minimums
AdmissionCan you get in?Each college's own admission standards
MoneyCan your family afford it?Academic merit aid, which can stack with athletic aid (and at Division III, it's the main aid there is)

A coach who loves your game still can't sign you if you're not eligible or can't be admitted. Strong grades mean a coach never has to wonder.

Not every class counts: core courses

For NCAA Divisions I and II, only core courses count toward eligibility. These are academic courses in English, math, natural or physical science, social science and a few others, like world languages. Your school has its own list of NCAA-approved core courses on the NCAA Eligibility Center website. Your counselor can show you where to find it.

Your core-course GPA uses only those courses. It's often different from your regular GPA, so you need to know both.

What each level asks for

LevelAcademic rule (as of 2026)
NCAA Division I16 core courses and a 2.3 core-course GPA. Ten of them, seven in English, math or science, must be done before your senior year starts (the 10/7 rule).
NCAA Division II16 core courses and a 2.2 core-course GPA.
NCAA Division IIINo NCAA academic minimum. Each college sets its own admission standards, and many are selective.
NAIAGraduate high school with a 2.30 GPA, or meet two of three: a 2.0 GPA, a minimum test score, or a top-half class rank.
Junior college (NJCAA)Graduate high school or earn an equivalency diploma.

The NCAA no longer requires SAT or ACT scores for eligibility, but some colleges still use them for admission or scholarships. Ask each school on your list.

Why ninth through eleventh grade matter most

Under the Division I 10/7 rule, the ten core courses you finish before senior year lock in when senior year starts. You can't go back and retake them to raise your GPA for eligibility. So the grades you earn now aren't practice. They count.

The minimum is just the floor. Coaches and admission offices look for students who are well above it.

Plan all four years

Sit down with your counselor and map your courses for all four years. Make sure you'll finish 16 core courses in the right subjects, on time. Then look past eligibility: which courses move you toward the career you're curious about? If you're thinking about nursing, engineering or business, the right math and science classes matter now.

The Academic Edge Planner does the math for you: it calculates your core-course GPA, checks your four-year plan against Division I and II rules, and saves your semester goal.

Set one goal for this semester

Pick one academic goal you can measure: raise a grade, finish a project early, meet with a teacher every week. Write it in your Tracker and check it at the end of the semester. Small academic habits, like the ones you built in Step 4, add up across four years.

Your moves for Step 5

  1. Find your school's approved core course list.
  2. Calculate your core-course GPA with your counselor.
  3. Compare your courses with each level's rules.
  4. Map your courses to your future goals.
  5. Set one academic goal in your Tracker.

8Family conversation guide Take-home · 15 min

Dear family, your student-athlete has started Level III, Pathways. Step 5 is about the part of recruiting they control most: academics.

The most important idea: grades open three doors: eligibility to compete, admission, and academic money. For NCAA Division I, the core courses finished before senior year lock in when senior year starts, so ninth- through eleventh-grade grades matter a great deal.

Questions to ask your student

  1. What's the difference between your regular GPA and your core-course GPA?
  2. Which levels are you considering, and what do they require academically?
  3. What's your academic goal this semester? How can we help?
  4. When are you meeting with your counselor about your four-year plan?

Your role this step: help your student meet with their counselor, and ask about the plan, not just the report card. If a class is a struggle, help them find support early (a teacher's help sessions, a tutor or a study group).

Money note: academic merit aid can stack with athletic aid, and at Division III it's the main aid available. Strong grades can lower your family's cost at many colleges.

9Coach talking points For head coaches · 5 min read

Say these three things

  1. “Your grades are the part of recruiting you control most. They decide whether you can compete, get in and get money.”
  2. “Only core courses count for NCAA eligibility, and for Division I, the grades you earn before senior year lock in.”
  3. “The minimum is the floor. I want to be able to tell a college coach you're well above it.”
