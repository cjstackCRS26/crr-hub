Weigh Your Options: Choose by Fit, Cost and Purpose

By Coach Stack · College Recruiting Standard

Draft for Coach Stack's review. New content written for Lesson 12, built on the framework's Step 12 actions and the Decision Lab. Rules and dates were checked in November 2026; confirm them each year.

The decision your whole journey builds toward

Eleven steps got you here. You know the landscape, you know your purpose, you've built a list, gotten seen and walked the campuses. Now the offers, or the options, are on the table. Step 12 is about choosing well: by fit, cost and purpose, not by the loudest coach, the biggest logo or whichever visit happened last.

Know what's actually on the table

Not every “offer” means the same thing. Before you compare schools, be clear about what each one is offering you:

LevelWhat an offer can include
NCAA Division IA roster spot and athletic aid, full or partial. Schools in the House settlement now have roster limits instead of scholarship limits, so roster spots can be tighter.
NCAA Division IIA roster spot and athletic aid, often partial and combined with academic aid.
NCAA Division IIIA roster spot and admissions support. No athletic scholarships; aid is merit-based or need-based.
NAIA and junior collegeA roster spot and athletic aid in many programs, each with its own agreements.

A roster spot with no aid is still a real offer. So is a strong academic scholarship at a school where you'd be a walk-on. Compare what's true, not what sounds biggest.

Get it in writing

A verbal offer or verbal commitment is a promise, not a contract. Either side can change its mind. The National Letter of Intent was retired in 2024; in NCAA Divisions I and II you now commit by signing a written athletics aid agreement. Once you sign one, your recruitment ends: other Division I and II coaches can no longer contact you.

Before you sign anything, ask:

  • How much aid is it, and for how many years? Is it renewed one year at a time?
  • What happens to my aid if I'm injured, or if the coach leaves?
  • What do I have to do to keep it: grades, conduct, staying on the team?
  • Will outside scholarships reduce the school's own aid?

Run the numbers: net cost

The sticker price isn't what you'll pay. The number that matters is net cost: the full cost of attendance minus grants and scholarships, the money you never pay back. Loans are not a discount; they're money you borrow. Compare every school on net cost per year, and then over four years.

That starts with the FAFSA. File it as soon as it opens in the fall of senior year; the 2027–28 form opened in late September 2026, the earliest ever. Most colleges use it to build your aid offer, athletes included, and some also ask for the CSS Profile. File it again every year you're in college.

Decide with a framework

When the choices are close, emotions run high and everyone has an opinion. A simple framework keeps the decision yours:

  1. Rank your top five factors. Academics and your major, the coaching staff, your role, team culture, campus life, career outcomes, net cost. Only you can put them in order.
  2. Score your finalists on those factors in a weighted table, with your campus visit scores beside them.
  3. Take the injury test: if you never played a game here, would you still want to be a student here?
  4. Check your purpose. Reread what you wrote in Step 3. Which option fits it best?
  5. Check your gut. If the scores and your gut disagree, find out why before you decide.

Talk it through with your family and your counselor or coach. Then make the call.

Commit with class

Once you decide, follow through: sign any written agreement, accept your admission and pay the enrollment deposit by the school's deadline (many schools use May 1). Then thank every coach who recruited you, including the ones you're turning down. Tell them yourself, kindly and soon. College sports is a small world, and how you say no is remembered.

Your moves for Step 12

  1. Complete the FAFSA as soon as it opens in the fall.
  2. Compare the net cost of every offer.
  3. Rank your top five factors and score your finalists.
  4. Review any written offer with your family and counselor.
  5. Decide, then thank every coach who recruited you.

10Family conversation guide Take-home · 20 min

Dear family, in Step 12 your student-athlete compares their options and makes their college decision, by fit, cost and purpose.

The most important idea: compare net cost, not sticker price or scholarship size. Grants and scholarships lower the cost; loans don't.

Do this together

  1. File the FAFSA as soon as it opens in the fall, and every year after.
  2. Lay out every offer side by side, in writing, with net cost per year and over four years.
  3. Ask your student to rank their top five factors before you share yours.
  4. Agree on a budget early, so no one falls in love with a school your family can't afford.

Your role this step: own the money conversation, and let your student own the choice.

11Coach talking points For head coaches · 5 min read

Say these three things

  1. “Choose the school you'd still choose if you never played a game there.”
  2. “Compare net cost, and get every offer in writing before you sign.”
  3. “Thank every coach who recruited you, including the ones you say no to.”

Questions you'll get

QuestionAnswer
“A coach says the offer expires Friday. What do I do?”Ask for the offer in writing and for a little more time to talk with family. A good fit is worth one honest conversation.
“Is a verbal commitment binding?”No. In Divisions I and II, signing a written athletics aid agreement is the commitment.
“How do I tell a coach no?”Call or email them yourself, soon. Thank them, tell them your decision, and wish them well.
