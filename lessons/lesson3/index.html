ARTICLE · ABOUT 7 MINUTES · DRAFT

Define Your Purpose: Know Yourself First

By Coach Stack · College Recruiting Standard

Draft for Coach Stack's review. New content written for Lesson 3, built on the framework's Step 3 actions, the Student-Athlete Discover Assessment and the Goal Stack Planner.

You've seen the map. Now look in the mirror.

In Step 2 you learned how big college sports really is: four associations, hundreds of colleges, around 700,000 athletes. That's a lot of options. And here's the catch: more options don't help you until you know what you're looking for.

That's what Step 3 is about. Before you pick colleges, email coaches or film a highlight video, you need to know who you are, what you're good at, and why you want this in the first place. Your purpose is the filter you'll run every later decision through.

Why “why” comes first

Recruiting takes years. There will be seasons when you don't get the playing time you want, coaches who don't write back, and a weekend tournament the night before a big test. When that happens, “because I'm supposed to” won't keep you going. A reason that's actually yours will.

Your why also protects you from chasing someone else's goal. A lot of freshmen say they want to play Division I because a parent, a teammate or a club coach said so. That goal might fit you. It might not. You won't know until you test it against what you really care about.

Start with your strengths

Purpose starts with honest self-knowledge. The Student-Athlete Discover Assessment walks you through four questions:

DimensionThe questionWhat you'll look at
PersonalWho am I as a person?Your character, your habits, the traits teammates would use to describe you
AcademicWhat kind of student am I?Your strongest subjects, how you learn, what you might want to study
AthleticWhat kind of player am I?Your three signature skills and two development priorities
EnvironmentWhat is around me?The people, resources and barriers that shape your path

When you finish, you pick your three defining strengths and two growth priorities. Those five words become the backbone of everything else in this Step.

Ask two other people

You don't see yourself the way others see you. Nobody does. So this Step asks you to go to your career advisor and one other adult (a coach, a teacher, a relative, a boss) and ask a simple question: “What do you think my three biggest strengths are?”

It can feel awkward. Do it anyway. Sometimes they'll name a strength you already listed, which tells you it's real. Sometimes they'll name one you never noticed, which is even better. Write down exactly what they say.

Find your why

Now the big question: what drives you to compete?

Your first answer is usually on the surface: “I love my sport.” Good. Now ask yourself why again. Why do you love it? Maybe it's the team. Why does the team matter? Maybe it's because you've never felt more like yourself than when you're working hard with people who count on you. Keep asking until you reach something that feels true, even a little personal.

Then write it down in one or two sentences. It doesn't need to be impressive. It needs to be honest. A why that's honest will outlast one that sounds good.

Stack your goals

A why without goals is a wish. In the Goal Stack Planner you build a three-layer pyramid:

  1. Your primary goal at the top: the one big thing you're working toward this year. You set it once a year.
  2. Milestones in the middle: the steps that prove you're on track. You check them each term.
  3. Habits at the base: what you do every week to get there. You check them every week.

The pyramid covers four commitments: your sport, your academics, your recruiting and yourself. The top of the pyramid gets the attention, but the base does the work. You'll build those weekly habits for real in Step 4.

Write your identity statement

Last, you put it all together in an identity statement: a short paragraph that says who you are as a student-athlete, right now. The Discover Assessment gives you a template:

“I am a [one word] student-athlete who brings [three strengths] to my team. Academically I am strongest in [subject]. On the field I am known for [skill]. Right now I am working to improve [growth area]. The opportunity I want most is [opportunity].”

Save it in your Tracker. You'll come back to it when you build your college list, when you write to your first coach, and when you're choosing between offers. Every time a decision gets hard, ask: does this fit who I said I am?

And it's allowed to change. You'll grow a lot between Grade 9 and Grade 12. Update it when you do.

Your moves for Step 3

  1. Complete the Student-Athlete Discover Assessment.
  2. Ask your career advisor and one other adult to name your strengths.
  3. Write your why in one or two sentences.
  4. Build your goal pyramid in the Goal Stack Planner.
  5. Save your identity statement in your Tracker.
“Know yourself first. Every good decision in recruiting starts there.”Coach Stack

3Mine or borrowed? Sort · 10 min

Read each reason. Is it Mine (it comes from what you value) or Borrowed (it comes from someone else)?

1. “I want to play in college because my older brother did, and everyone expects me to.”

It's someone else's expectation. It could become yours, but only once you test it.

2. “I love competing with a team, and I want four more years of it.”

It comes from your own experience of the sport.

3. “Coach says I'm a D1 player, so that's my goal.”

Good input, but it's the coach's read. Test it against your own strengths and what you want.

4. “I want to study nursing and keep playing, so I'm looking for schools where I can do both.”

It joins your own academic and athletic goals.

5. “I want a scholarship offer so I can post it.”

It's about how others will see you, not what you want from college.

6. “I want to be the first in my family to graduate from college, and my sport can help get me there.”

It's personal, specific and yours.

7. “All my friends are going to the same school, so I want to go there too.”

Friends matter, but the choice should fit you.

8. “I want to see how good I can get if I keep working.”

It's driven by your own growth.

The point to land: borrowed goals aren't bad, and plenty of them turn out to fit. But a goal only holds up through hard seasons once you've tested it and made it yours.

8Family conversation guide Take-home · 15 min

Dear family, this week your student-athlete started Level II, Discover. After learning the full landscape of college sports in Step 2, they're now turning inward: naming their strengths, finding their why, setting goals and writing an identity statement that describes who they are as a student-athlete right now.

The most important idea: a goal has to be theirs to last. Recruiting takes years, and your student's own reasons are what keep them going when it gets hard.

Questions to ask your student

  1. What do you think your three biggest strengths are? (Then share one you see in them.)
  2. Why do you play? What do you love most about it?
  3. What's one goal you have for this year: in your sport, in school, or for yourself?
  4. Is there any goal you feel like you're carrying for someone else?

Your role this step: listen more than you talk. If your student's goal is different from what you imagined, ask questions before offering opinions. You may be one of the two adults your student asks to name their strengths; answer honestly and specifically.

What to skip this year: picking a college or a division for them. That comes later, once your student's why and strengths point the way.

9Coach talking points For head coaches · 5 min read

Say these three things

  1. “Before you pick colleges, know who you are. Your strengths and your why are the filter for every recruiting decision.”
  2. “Strengths aren't only athletic. College coaches recruit people: character, academics and how you treat teammates count.”
  3. “A goal you've borrowed from someone else breaks first. Test it and make it yours.”
