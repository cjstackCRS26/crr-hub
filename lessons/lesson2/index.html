Know the Landscape: College Sports Is Bigger Than You Think

By Coach Stack · College Recruiting Standard

Draft for Coach Stack's review. Figures from the Step 2 toolkit sources: NFHS 2024–25 participation survey and NCAA 2024–25 participation and probability data.

“I want to play D1.”

Almost every freshman athlete has said it. Fewer can tell you how many college athletes actually play Division I, or what the other levels of college sports look like. That's what this Step is for: before you plan anything, you need the whole map.

Here's what nobody tells freshmen. College sports is a lot bigger than what you see on TV. Most high school athletes will not play in college. But far more students could compete in college than think they can, because most college opportunity is outside Division I.

The numbers

In 2024–25, 8,266,244 high school students played a sport, a national record. About 700,000 students compete in college athletics, roughly 8.5% of high school athletes. About 204,000 of them compete in NCAA Division I, about 2.5% of high school athletes.

Read that again: Division I is less than a third of all college athletes. It's the level everyone talks about, and the smallest slice of the map.

Divisions II and III together are bigger than Division I, and the NAIA and junior colleges add more than 140,000 athletes on top of that.

Opportunity is not the same as scholarship

If you remember one thing from this Step, make it this: athletic opportunity is not the same thing as athletic scholarship.

Only about 2% of high school athletes receive an athletic scholarship, and most of those awards are partial. Full rides are rare. That's not bad news; it just means scholarship is the wrong measuring stick. Division III has more colleges than any other NCAA division, and it gives no athletic scholarships at all. Its athletes compete anyway, many with academic and need-based aid.

So if you think “no scholarship means no college sports,” you just crossed off the NCAA's biggest division without knowing it.

Your sport has its own numbers

Every sport is different. In men's lacrosse, about one in seven high school players goes on to compete in the NCAA. In men's basketball, it's closer to one in thirty. And those numbers count the NCAA only. The NAIA and junior colleges sponsor many of the same sports, so your real odds of competing somewhere are higher.

That's why this Step's digital tool is My Sport Landscape. Pick your sport and you'll see the levels, the real odds, the roster sizes and the contact rules for your sport, all in one place.

Recruiting has its own language, and its own rules

You'll hear words like contact, evaluation, dead period and official visit. You don't need to memorize them today; the full list is in the toolkit below. But five guidelines are worth knowing now:

  1. The rules limit coaches, not you. You can email a coach, fill out a questionnaire or go to a camp at any age.
  2. Silence is not a verdict. In most Division I sports, coaches can't email, text or call you until June 15 after your sophomore year. A coach who doesn't reply before then is following the rules.
  3. Verbal is not binding. Verbal offers and verbal commitments can change. Only a signed athletics aid agreement binds, and it replaced the National Letter of Intent in 2024.
  4. Rules differ by level and sport, and change every year. Division II starts earlier than Division I; Division III and the NAIA have far fewer restrictions. Use the official sources, not a teammate's memory.
  5. Violations belong to the college. If a coach contacts you too early, you're not penalized, but it tells you something about the program.

A better question

Stop asking “Can I play D1?” Ask this instead: “What opportunity is right for me, and what do I need to do to get ready for it?” That question has an answer for every athlete in your locker room.

RECRUITING TERMS

Recruiting has its own language

  • Contact: a face-to-face meeting with a college coach, beyond a simple hello.
  • Evaluation: a coach watching you compete or visiting your school.
  • Contact / dead period: when coaches may, or may not, meet you in person.
  • Unofficial / official visit: you pay, or the college pays.
  • Verbal offer: a coach saying they want you. Not binding.
  • Athletics aid agreement: the signed, binding offer. Replaced the National Letter of Intent in 2024.

RECRUITING GUIDELINES

When can college coaches reach out?

  • DI NCAA Division I Calls and messages June 15 after sophomore year; off-campus contact and official visits August 1 before junior year
  • DII NCAA Division II Materials any time; calls and contact June 15 before junior year
  • DIII NCAA Division III No date limit on calls and messages; off-campus contact after sophomore year
  • NAIA NAIA Any time; no recruiting calendar

4Opportunity vs. Scholarship Sort · 10 min

Sort each card: is it an Opportunity (a chance to compete in college), a Scholarship (money for athletic ability), Both, or Neither alone?

1. A roster spot on a Division III team

Division III gives no athletic scholarships, but the spot is real.

2. A partial athletic award at a Division II college

It is an opportunity that comes with athletic money.

3. Playing at a junior college for two years

A real path, and a second chance to be recruited by four-year programs.

4. An academic merit award at a college where you also make the team

The money is for grades, not athletics; the roster spot is the opportunity.

5. A full athletic scholarship at a Division I college

Rare, but it is both.

6. Walking on to a team without athletic money

Competing without an athletic award is still competing.

7. Need-based aid from the FAFSA

It pays for college; it is not tied to athletics.

8. An athletic award at an NAIA college

The NAIA awards athletic scholarships.

The point to land: the Opportunity pile is far bigger than the Scholarship pile. About 2% of high school athletes receive athletic scholarships, but about 8.5% compete in college.

7Family conversation guide Take-home · 15 min

Dear family, this week your student-athlete learned how big the world of college sports really is. About 700,000 students compete in college athletics across four associations: the NCAA (Divisions I, II and III), the NAIA, the NJCAA (junior colleges) and the USCAA. Division I is less than a third of them.

The most important idea: athletic opportunity is not the same thing as athletic scholarship. About 2% of high school athletes receive athletic scholarships, and most are partial. Division III, the NCAA's largest division, offers no athletic scholarships, yet its athletes compete with academic and need-based aid.

Questions to ask your student

  1. What surprised you most about college sports today?
  2. How many levels of college athletics play your sport?
  3. What's the difference between an athletic opportunity and an athletic scholarship?
  4. What question do you still have?

Your role this year: support the process; don't become the process. In Grade 9, students learn the map and discover their purpose. Contacting college coaches, camps and showcases come later, in Grades 10 and 11.

Two rules worth knowing now. NCAA recruiting rules limit what college coaches can do, not what your student can do. And a verbal offer or verbal commitment is not binding; only a signed athletics aid agreement is.

Trusted, free sources: NCAA Eligibility Center (ncaa.org) · NAIA (playnaia.org) · NJCAA (njcaa.org) · Federal Student Aid and the FAFSA (studentaid.gov). You do not need to pay a recruiting service this year.
