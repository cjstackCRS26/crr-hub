ARTICLE · ABOUT 8 MINUTES · DRAFT

Prepare for the Transition: Finish Strong, Arrive Ready

By Coach Stack · College Recruiting Standard

Draft for Coach Stack's review. New content written for Lesson 13, built on the framework's Step 13 actions. Eligibility rules were checked in November 2026 (NCAA Division I adopted its age-based model in June 2026 and Division II in August 2026); confirm them each year.

You've decided. Now finish strong.

Signing day feels like the finish line. It isn't. The months between your decision and your first day on campus decide how ready you arrive, and the first year of college is where many student-athletes struggle. Step 13 is about protecting everything you've built: your eligibility, your grades and your wellbeing.

Senior spring still counts

Your admission and your aid depend on finishing high school the way you started senior year. Colleges see your final transcript, and an offer of admission can be withdrawn if grades fall sharply. Finish your remaining courses, including any you need for NCAA eligibility, and graduate on time.

Close out the paperwork

Every college and every level has its own checklist. The big pieces:

WhatWhen and how
Final transcript with proof of graduationAfter you graduate, ask your counselor to send it to your college and, for NCAA Division I or II, upload it to your Eligibility Center account. NAIA students send it to the NAIA Eligibility Center.
Final amateurism certification (NCAA Division I and II)Request it in your Eligibility Center account on or after April 1 before fall enrollment (October 1 for winter or spring).
Financial aidAccept your aid offer, finish anything the aid office asks for, and remember the FAFSA is filed again every year.
Housing and orientationPay any housing deposit, choose an orientation date and register for classes.
Athletics paperworkComplete every form the athletic department sends, including the preparticipation physical, before you report.

Don't assume someone else has done it. Confirm your eligibility, aid and housing with each office yourself.

Know your eligibility clock

The rules changed in 2026. In NCAA Divisions I and II, you now have five years to play up to five seasons. Your clock starts when you first enroll full time in college or at the start of the academic year after your 19th birthday, whichever comes first, and it keeps running. Redshirt years and injury extensions are gone; the only exceptions are pregnancy, active-duty military service and official religious missions. That makes every year count, and it means a gap year can cost you a season. Division III, the NAIA and junior colleges have their own rules: ask the compliance office at your school.

Use the summer

Ask your new coach for the summer training plan and follow it. Arrive fit, because the first weeks of preseason are often the hardest of the year. Reach out to a few future teammates. Learn the names of the people who will support you: your academic advisor, the athletic trainer, the tutoring center, the counseling center and the career center.

What's different in year one

College gives you more freedom and more responsibility at the same time. No one will remind you about assignments. Practice, travel, lifts and classes fill your week, and you may play less than you did in high school, at least at first. Homesickness is normal. The students who do best plan their week, go to class every time, meet professors early and ask for help before they need it. If you're struggling in any way, academically, physically or emotionally, tell someone you trust. Every campus has support for exactly this.

Look back, then pay it forward

Before you leave, take time to look back at your journey: where you started, what you learned and who helped you. Thank the people on your recruiting team. Then pay it forward: the younger athletes at your school are where you were four years ago. Your story can help them start sooner.

Your moves for Step 13

  1. Request your final transcript and proof of graduation.
  2. Confirm your eligibility and aid with each office.
  3. Get your summer training plan from your new coach.
  4. Attend orientation and register for classes.
  5. Complete your exit reflection.

10Family conversation guide Take-home · 15 min

Dear family, in Step 13 your student-athlete finishes high school strong and gets ready for college: closing out paperwork, following a summer plan and preparing for year one.

The most important idea: signing day isn't the finish line. Grades, paperwork and preparation in these months protect everything your student has earned.

Do this together

  1. Make a shared list of every deadline: transcript, certification, aid, housing, orientation, athletics forms.
  2. Talk honestly about what year one may feel like: less playing time, more independence, homesickness.
  3. Agree on how you'll stay in touch, and on who your student can go to on campus.

Your role this step: step back. Let your student make the calls and send the emails. Be the first person they call, not the first person who calls.

11Coach talking points For head coaches · 5 min read

Say these three things

  1. “Signing day isn't the finish line. Finish your senior year the way you started it.”
  2. “Get your summer plan and follow it. Arrive ready.”
  3. “Ask for help early. It's what the best college athletes do.”

Questions you'll get

QuestionAnswer
“Should I play my spring sport after I've signed?”Ask your new coach. Many encourage it; some want you to focus on their training plan.
“Can I still redshirt?”Not in NCAA Divisions I and II under the 2026 rules. You have five years to play up to five seasons, and the clock keeps running.
“How can I give back?”Talk to younger athletes about your journey. Your story helps them start sooner.
