Share Your Story: Reach Out, Respond, Follow Up

By Coach Stack · College Recruiting Standard

Draft for Coach Stack's review. New content written for Lesson 9, built on the framework's Step 9 actions and the Communication Lab. Contact rules were checked in November 2026; confirm your sport's dates each year.

Coaches recruit students who reach out

Welcome to Level V, Exposure & Engagement. You've built your brand. Now it's time to use it. College coaches recruit students who reach out, respond and follow up. Waiting to be discovered isn't a plan. For most athletes, the first move has to be theirs.

How you communicate tells a coach a lot about you: whether you're organized, whether you follow through and whether you've done your homework on their program. In many cases, a coach will form an opinion of your maturity from your emails long before they see you play.

Five channels for telling your story

ChannelWhat it does
QuestionnairesMost college team websites have a recruiting questionnaire. Filling it out puts you in the program's system.
EmailYour main way to introduce yourself, share your schedule and follow up.
Phone and video callsWhere a coach hears how you think and who you are.
Your recruiting profileThe page your emails point to: bio, video, academics and schedule, kept current.
Social mediaA public account that shows your training and character, audited in Step 8.

Start with the questionnaires

For each school on your target list, find the recruiting questionnaire on the team's page of the college athletics website and fill it out completely. It takes about ten minutes a school, and it's often how a program first starts tracking you.

Write emails that only one program could receive

Coaches get a lot of emails. Most look the same: “Dear Coach, I am very interested in your program.” They can tell a copy-and-paste in one line.

A good introduction email is short, around 120 to 200 words, and it's specific:

  1. A clear subject line: your grad year, position, club or high school, and something specific, like an upcoming event.
  2. Who you are: name, grad year, position, school and club.
  3. Why this program: something true only of them: their style of play, a major they offer, something a current player said.
  4. What you bring: one or two strengths, with your academics.
  5. Your links and next step: your video, your profile, your schedule, and one question a coach can answer.

Write it yourself. Coaches want to hear your voice, not a parent's. The Communication Lab walks you through each part and checks the length, but it never writes the email for you.

Know when coaches can write back

You can email college coaches any time. Some coaches have limits on when they can reply. Division II and Division III coaches can call, text and email you at any time. In most Division I sports, coaches can start direct recruiting communication on June 15 after your sophomore year, and a few sports use different dates. NAIA and junior college coaches have far fewer limits. If a Division I coach doesn't reply before your sport's date, they may simply be following the rules. (In-person, off-campus contact and visits have their own dates at every NCAA level.)

Follow up with something new

No reply after two weeks? Follow up, but don't just ask “Did you get my email?” Lead with what's new: a new video, a tournament result, your grades, an event where they can see you play. A follow-up with nothing new is a reminder, and coaches skip reminders.

Practice the phone call

At some point a coach will call. Be ready for the questions they almost always ask: tell me about yourself, why our program, what's your GPA, what are you working on, and do you have any questions for me? (Always say yes.) Practice a mock call with your counselor or coach, using your 30-second pitch from Step 8.

Log every contact

Every email you send, every reply, every call goes in your Coach Log on the dashboard: the date, the school, the coach, what was said and when to follow up. Recruiting conversations run across months and dozens of schools. The log is how you stay organized, and it's one of the Three Habits in action.

Your moves for Step 9

  1. Complete the questionnaire for each school on your list.
  2. Email three coaches, each message written for that program.
  3. Practice a coach phone call with your counselor or coach.
  4. Update your recruiting profile and social media.
  5. Log every contact and reply in your Tracker.

8When can coaches write back? Check · 5 min

You can email any coach, any time. Coaches have limits on when they can reply:

  • NCAA Division II and III: calls, texts and emails any time
  • Most NCAA Division I sports: June 15 after sophomore year
  • A few Division I sports differ, such as women's basketball, men's ice hockey and lacrosse
  • NAIA and junior college: far fewer limits

Rules as of November 2026. Check the NCAA website or ask your counselor each year.

10Family conversation guide Take-home · 15 min

Dear family, your student-athlete has started Level V, Exposure & Engagement. In Step 9 they begin communicating with college coaches: filling out questionnaires, emailing three coaches, practicing a phone call and logging every contact.

The most important idea: coaches recruit students who reach out, respond and follow up, and they want to hear from your student, in your student's own words.

Questions to ask your student

  1. Which three coaches are you emailing first? Why those?
  2. What's specific about each email?
  3. When can coaches in your sport write back?
  4. Want to practice a coach phone call with me?

Your role this step: proofread, don't write. Make sure your student's email address is professional and checked daily. If a coach calls the house, take a message and let your student call back.

Patience: no reply doesn't always mean no interest. Some Division I coaches aren't allowed to reply yet, and all coaches are busy.

11Coach talking points For head coaches · 5 min read

Say these three things

  1. “Coaches recruit players who reach out. Write the first email yourself.”
  2. “Make every email specific to that program. If it could go to any school, rewrite it.”
  3. “Follow up with something new, and log every contact.”
